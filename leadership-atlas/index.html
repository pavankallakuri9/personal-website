What performance and leadership mean

Al Khajeh 2018 · Knies, Jacobsen & Tummers 2016
Performance

Results measured against mission

The results and outputs of an organisation, measured against its mission and objectives. Optimally performing organisations are efficient, productive, competitive and sustainable.

Leadership

A social process of influence

Leaders deliberately exert influence over followers to enable group activity. At its core it is about dyadic, paired relationships between leader and follower. Over time those qualities enter the organisation's collective consciousness.

Tim Morris · two ways to define effectiveness1) Making the organisation, or your part of it, survive and prosper. 2) Meeting the demands of stakeholders: customers, shareholders, regulators, employees.

Three primary styles

Lewin, Lippitt & White 1939

Schoolchildren doing arts and crafts were placed under three kinds of leader. The differences in output were traced to the leader's style, and all three styles are still visible in organisations today.

Authoritarian / autocratic

Strict leader–follower divide

  • Total authority; no input sought
  • Compliance via rewards and punishments
  • Linked to high turnover and absenteeism

Works when followers are inexperienced, time is short, or coordination with others is needed.

Participatory / democratic

High engagement

  • Leader guides; followers share decisions
  • In the study, less productive but higher quality
  • Motivated, creative, cooperative teams

Note: it is much harder to move from autocratic to democratic than the reverse.

Delegative / laissez-faire

Hands-off

  • Followers set goals and solve problems
  • In the study, least productive; more demands on leader
  • Lacked direction and ownership

Works when leading highly educated people who need little direction.

Situational and contingency leadership

How to recognise ineffective leadership

Julie Nerney

It looks inauthentic

Usually for one of three reasons:

  • Thinking of self, not the collective
  • Too task-focused, people neglected
  • Poor cultural fit, e.g. dictatorial in a consensus culture
Michael Gill

“Ineffective for whom?”

If effective leadership is organising a group to set and achieve goals, judgement depends on who is judging and when. People also judge how a goal was achieved, not just whether.

Steve Mostyn

Misdiagnosis

The number one failure is framing the issue wrongly: the wrong diagnosis of what the organisation needs to grow and flourish.

How ideas about effective leadership evolved

Michael Gill

Great man / trait

19th century: effective leaders have traits such as decisiveness, intelligence, strength.

Learnable skills

Leadership can be taught.

Contingency

Context decides what works.

Charisma & EI

1960s–70s onward: charismatic, emotional and emotionally intelligent leadership.

TakeawayEven “obviously good” traits can hinder. Sometimes indecision is a boon. So be clear about what you are trying to do and how you will define success for your constituents: shareholders, staff, wider society.

What effective leaders do

A leader only has one job to do, and that's to create an environment which lets everybody be the best version of themselves.

Julie Nerney
Be clear on the “what”

Goals, objectives, priorities.

Invest in the “how”

Ways of working and culture. The “how” is a performance accelerant and matters more than the “what”.

Strong feedback loops

Constantly seek feedback; visibly committed to learning.

Balance

Confidence + humility

Enough confidence to lead, enough humility to know you don't have all the answers, and the self-awareness to keep improving.

Steve Mostyn

Find the real work, together

Leadership is not the preserve of executives. Do the diagnostic work with the team, then put pressure on the system to align resources. Build leadership capability system-wide (about 170 FE college CEOs through one programme), and manage the transitions: line manager → director → general manager.

Context is king

Gill

Context is dynamic. Followers' desires and interests change over time, so effective leadership must change with them.

Nerney · lifecycle

Distress, turnaround, steady state, growth, transformation, M&A. Each needs a different what and how. Keep asking: what will I start, stop, continue?

Mostyn

Insiders carry assumptions about “how things work here”. Step back and challenge the orthodoxy. A founder becoming a scale-up leader is a major transition.

Strengths, weaknesses and derailment

Gill · reflexivity

Routines get stale

The routines that made you successful are seductive and can become outdated. Reflect, seek alternative perspectives, and go further: ask how you shape, and are shaped by, the people around you.

Nerney

Two sides of one coin

Sometimes ignore a weakness and make a strength great; sometimes the weakness is core and must be fixed. Never stop learning.

Mostyn · derailment research

Overplaying strengths

The number one predictor of derailment: under pressure in a big role, the ex-marketeer becomes a “super marketeer”. Recruit around your weaknesses (e.g. a great CFO who explains simply).

Defining vision

Vision is the desired future state: a clear mental image of what you want to achieve and where to steer. Without one an organisation may survive, but it is unlikely to excel or grow, because there is no way to prioritise decisions.

Authentic leadership

George 2003, 2016 · Avolio & Gardner 2005 · Walumbwa et al. 2007

From the Greek authentikos, genuine. Authenticity means leading adaptively from your core: true to unchanging values, honest, ethical and practical. Character takes precedence over skills and style.

Bill George · authentic leaders…
  • Understand how their life story shaped them
  • Regularly reflect and introspect
  • Seek honest feedback
  • Align personal purpose with the organisation's
  • Adapt their style to context
Avolio · how influence works

Leaders draw on positive psychological capital (confidence, optimism, hope, resilience) through two mechanisms:

  • Personal & social identification: role modelling aligns values with mission
  • Positive emotions: hope, trust and optimism raise satisfaction, commitment and meaning

Result: optimised performance and willingness to go the extra mile.

Four core elements

Self-awareness

Know your strengths and biases and how you affect others. A constant learning process.

Internalised moral perspective

Do the right thing from your own values, not external pressure.

Relational transparency

Genuine, no ulterior motives. Open and coherent, without disclosing every thought.

Balanced processing

Invite opposing views; put the organisation's interest above your own.

French & Raven's five forms of power

French & Raven 1959 · Lunenburg 2012

Power is the ability to cause a psychological change in the behaviour, beliefs, values and goals of others. It creates dyadic relationships of interdependence and reciprocity.

Positional · in the role

Legitimate

Formal authority to direct work within recognised duties. Narrow sphere; inappropriate outside it.

Reward

Bonuses, raises, promotions, desirable work. Works if valued and clearly linked to behaviour. Followers can hold it too, via feedback systems.

Coercive

Reprimands, bad assignments, suspension, dismissal. Curbed by policy and unions. Peers can coerce informally through sarcasm and exclusion.

Personal · in the person

Expert

Specialist knowledge, credentials, trustworthiness. Growing in value; even junior experts get consulted.

Referent

Respect and admiration from style, charisma and reputation. People want to please and emulate you.

Three more sources (Astley & Sachdeva 1984)

Information

Holding knowledge others need to do their work.

Control of resources

Scarce resources essential to the organisation.

Networks

The more central your position in the web of relationships, the more indispensable you become.

Which way power flows

Yukl & Falbe 1991
Typical pattern

Positional power is used downward on followers; personal power on peers and external stakeholders.

The cost of positional power

Overused authority breeds frustration and resistance. Rewards create dependence and can feel manipulative. Coercion alienates and drives poor performance and turnover.

Why personal power wins

It drives satisfaction, performance and commitment, and builds a “climate of trust” where influence is internalised and surveillance is minimal.

Best combinationLegitimate + expert + referent is highly effective. Leaning on coercion and rewards can erode your personal power.

Controlling the agenda, resource allocation, and which options are never even considered.

Departmental power

Sits with units that control valuable resources or solve critical problems: finance (money) and sales (customers).

Aligning personal and organisational purpose
Beyond Friedman's profit doctrine

50 years of profit-first purpose brought climate change, inequality, burnout and unethical behaviour. Purpose means profitable products for people and planet, but profit is not the purpose. Tools: the Mountain to Mountain visualisation, and members bringing images that speak to the organisation's purpose.

Don't bend the organisation into your shape

Create space through culture, structure and process for others to find meaning, then get out of the way. Listen to uncomfortable things, make space for dissent, ask generative questions (“What might be possible if we…?”), and walk the talk.

Case · IKEAIn 2015 IKEA switched from halogen to LED bulbs, a potential cost of around €2 billion. The decision was made by the head of lighting, not the CEO, using the purpose “make the everyday better” and a purpose infrastructure that involves customers and suppliers.
