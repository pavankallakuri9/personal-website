Nigeria's textile & apparel industry: market, mills, trade and where to invest

Executive summary

A US$6–7 billion market, more than 90% supplied from abroad, with almost no working mill capacity left

Nigeria consumes an estimated US$6.5 billion (≈₦8.7 trillion) of textiles and apparel a year at retail value, range US$5.5–8.0 billion Medium. Official statistics capture only a fraction of the supply: the National Bureau of Statistics recorded ₦1.06 trillion (≈US$0.7 billion) of textile imports in 2025 [S3], while Chinese customs alone reported roughly US$100 million a month of one product line, synthetic filament woven fabric, shipped to Nigeria in mid-2025 [S19]. Our reconciliation puts true supply at the border at about US$3.4 billion (range 2.6–4.2) before distribution margins.

Domestic manufacturing is close to collapse. Industry bodies count roughly 20 surviving mills, many barely running, against 150–180 at the 1980s peak, and fewer than 20,000 mill workers [S4]. The flagship United Nigerian Textiles mill in Kaduna stopped in 2022 [S11]. Seed-cotton output has fallen to about 10,000–15,000 tonnes [S9] [S42], too little to feed a single modern spinning mill at scale.

The economics follow from three structural facts: Nigerian industrial power costs two to six times what Asian or Beninese competitors pay once diesel back-up is included; local cotton and synthetic fibre are not available at scale; and the duty and levy wall on imports is porous, because much cloth enters under-declared or through Benin and Niger. Greenfield spinning and weaving therefore fail our hurdle rate today. Investments that sit closer to the customer pass it.

Recommendation in one paragraph

Enter through distribution, then convert. Build a branded, compliant fabric import-and-wholesale platform in Lagos, Kano and Onitsha (≈US$4m capex plus ≈US$15m working capital; base IRR ≈31%), and add two cut-and-sew plants that carry low capital and protected demand: institutional uniforms and workwear (≈US$9m; IRR ≈21% with anchor contracts under Executive Order 003 and "Nigeria First") and home textiles and hospitality linen (≈US$6m; IRR ≈27%). Hold an option on a print, dye and finishing house (≈US$22m) until the platform proves volume and enforcement of the landed-cost wall improves; on today's prices it returns ≈12%, below an 18% US$ hurdle. Avoid greenfield spinning, integrated mega-mills and large brownfield rehabilitations with inherited labour liabilities. Total committed capital in the first 36 months: about US$35–45 million, scalable to US$100 million by Year 5 if the print house is triggered and an ECOWAS-origin sourcing leg (for example through Benin's Glo-Djigbé zone) is added.

Research methodology

How the numbers were built, and how far to trust them

This is a desktop study completed on 17 October 2026. It draws on published NBS trade releases as reported by the Nigerian press, Central Bank and Ministry of Finance circulars, UN Comtrade and WITS extracts, Chinese customs (GACC) figures, industry-association statements, company material, academic work and credible trade publications. No interviews or factory visits were carried out; Part 18 sets out the field programme that must precede any capital commitment.

Evidence classes used throughout

  • Market research evidence: official statistics and named-source statements, cited with [S#] links to the bibliography.
  • Expert inference: triangulations and conversions, shown in ochre and labelled with a confidence grade.
  • Preliminary financial modelling: desktop estimates in constant 2026 US dollars, not engineering quotations or audited cases.

Known limits

  • NBS publishes textile imports only as a section total in naira (HS Section XI, chapters 50–63); product and origin detail rely on Comtrade mirrors.
  • Nigeria's own Comtrade filings lag and are incomplete for 2024–2026.
  • Company capacities and revenues are rarely published; where we could not verify them we say so rather than estimate.
  • Historical naira-to-dollar conversions use our approximations of annual average official rates (Appendix A1).
So what?

The single largest analytical risk is the size of unrecorded supply. Every downstream conclusion holds across our range (US$2.6–4.2bn at the border), but the field programme should confirm it with importer and clearing-agent interviews before sizing any plant.

Volume I · Part 1

Executive industry overview

Where the industry came from

Nigeria's first large mill, Kaduna Textiles Limited, began production in 1957, and the Hong Kong-based Cha Group opened United Nigerian Textiles in Kaduna in 1964 [S13]. Import restrictions in the 1960s–70s drew investment, and by the late 1970s and 1980s about 167 mills employed more than 500,000 people, making the sector the second-largest employer after government [S51]. Clusters formed in Kaduna, Kano, Lagos (Isolo, Ikeja, Ikorodu), Aba, Asaba and Benin City [S16].

Employment and economic weight

Mill employment is below 20,000 by the union's count [S4]. Across tailoring, trading and cotton farming the value chain still employs millions, but no reliable census exists; we estimate 2–4 million livelihoods, mostly informal tailors and market traders Low. NBS values the textile, apparel and footwear activity at ₦8.15 trillion at current prices for 2024 [S34], and the CTG component at ₦4.38 trillion for 2025 [S9]. These large values reflect informal tailoring in the national accounts, not mill output.

So what?

Demand is large, stable and culturally anchored, but almost all value added upstream of the tailor is captured abroad. An investor is buying into a distribution and conversion problem, not a fibre-to-fabric one.

Units and conversion assumptions

  • Metres to kilograms: West African prints are sold in 6-yard pieces (5.49 m) at ≈112–120 cm width. At 110–140 g/m² a linear metre weighs 130–165 g; we use 150 g, i.e. ≈6.7 linear metres per kg. Lace (heavier, embroidered) ≈4 m/kg; polyester chiffon ≈12 m/kg.
  • Square metres: linear metres × 1.15 m average width.
  • Garments to fabric: 1.3 linear metres and ≈0.22 kg per average garment, so 1.07bn new and used garments ≈1.4bn metres-equivalent.
  • Total fabric-equivalent: 1.70bn m tailoring + ≈1.4bn m in garments + ≈0.3bn m home textiles ≈ 3.4bn linear metres (≈3.9bn m²), ≈14 m per capita.
  • Fibre content: ≈950kt including technical textiles, ≈4.0 kg per capita, versus ≈13–15 kg world average.

So what?

The three methods agree on a US$6–7bn market. The profit pools an entrant can access fastest are mid-tier prints (≈US$600m), home textiles (≈US$650m), lace (≈US$420m) and institutional uniforms (fabric plus garments, ≈US$400m), each almost wholly imported.

Fabric categories, usage and consumer behaviour

Nigeria remains a "buy cloth, see the tailor" market. Most adult ceremonial and much everyday womenswear is made to measure from fabric bought by the 6-yard piece or 5-yard lace length, while T-shirts, jeans, underwear, children's wear and sportswear are bought finished, new or second-hand.

Changing preferences

  • Income: the 2023–25 cost-of-living shock pushed buyers toward cheaper polyester wax-look prints and used clothing; with inflation easing to 14.6% [S30], a partial recovery of mid-tier cotton prints is plausible but unproven.
  • Age: under-30s buy more RMG and streetwear and use Instagram designers and small-batch "made in Nigeria" labels; older consumers remain loyal to tailored traditional dress.
  • Region: the north favours shadda, voile, chiffon and modest cuts with motif restrictions (premium brands avoid animal or cross motifs there [S41]); the south-west buys aso-oke and lace; the south-east buys george and lace.
  • Local pride: local brands such as Merok (Onchek, founded 2016) are reported to be gaining ground under the "Nigeria First" policy [S10]; scale is unknown.
So what?

Because most cloth is tailored, the cloth itself is the brand-bearing product. A trusted fabric brand with consistent colour-fastness and width can command a premium; a garment factory, by contrast, sells mainly to institutions and modern retail.

So what?

There is no large operating mill to buy. What this means for spinning and weaving

A single modern 50,000-spindle cotton mill consumes about 10–12kt of lint a year, two to three times Nigeria's entire current lint output. A spinning investment would therefore import lint (from Benin, Burkina Faso, Mali or Côte d'Ivoire; Benin alone produced 1.15 million bales in 2025/26 [S35]) and face the same energy disadvantage as weaving. Backward integration into cotton is a 5–7 year programme that requires guaranteed offtake, seed and input finance and ginning contracts; it should follow, not precede, proven downstream demand.

So what?

Plan on imported greige cloth, yarn and synthetic fibre for at least five years. Design any plant to switch to regional ECOWAS cotton inputs, which also secure duty-free origin status inside the bloc.

Ranking suppliers on the evidence

  1. China — an estimated 75–80% of supply. An academic study puts China's share of textiles in Nigerian markets at about 80% [S50]; Chinese line data show Nigeria as a top-two market for synthetic woven fabrics.
  2. India — yarns, cotton fabrics, lace, home textiles; India's High Commission reports active trade promotion in 2026 [S7b].
  3. Benin and other ECOWAS origins — re-exports of Asian cloth and used clothing via Cotonou, plus genuine ECOWAS-origin goods (GDIZ garments; Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire wax prints).
  4. Europe — premium lace (Austria, Switzerland), Vlisco wax (Netherlands), used clothing.
  5. Türkiye, Indonesia, Pakistan, Bangladesh — garments, knit fabrics, denim, home textiles; individually small in recorded data.

Transit and informal trade through Benin

Cotonou's port and markets serve as a regional wholesale hub. Some trade into Nigeria is legal (ECOWAS-origin goods enter under the trade liberalisation scheme), some is transit of third-country goods that should pay Nigerian duty, and some is outright smuggling, notably used clothing, which Nigeria has banned since the 1970s [S47]. Benin is also building genuine capacity: the Glo-Djigbé Industrial Zone processes about 40,000 tonnes of Beninese cotton into 7–10 million garments a year [S35], and Nigeria's Vice President led a delegation there in July 2026 to study its integrated model [S34]. We estimate informal and transit trade at US$0.5–1.0bn a year at border value, by difference between demand and recorded plus mirror-gap supply Low.

So what?

The real competitor of any Nigerian producer is a Chinese mill whose cloth often reaches Lagos with less than the full 35% duty, levy and VAT paid. Investment cases must be stress-tested at the "effective" landed cost, not the statutory one. A compliant importer with consistent quality and clean invoices can also win institutional and modern-trade buyers who cannot use informal supply.

High-value interview questions

Importers and brokers

  1. How many containers of each fabric category do you clear per month, and how has that changed since 2024?
  2. What duty, levies and VAT do you actually pay per container, as a percentage of invoice value?
  3. What share of your goods enters through Cotonou or land borders?
  4. What payment terms do your Chinese and Indian suppliers give, and what do you give wholesalers?
  5. What are your landed costs per metre for your three best sellers?
  6. How quickly do designs need to change to keep shelf space?
  7. Which buyers require formal invoices and SONCAP-compliant goods?
  8. What would make you buy from a Nigerian printer instead of importing?

Wholesalers and retailers

  1. Which ten items sell the most by volume and by value?
  2. What are your buy and sell prices per 6-yard piece by grade today?
  3. How many days of credit do you take and give?
  4. How do customers judge quality (weight, colour-fastness, selvedge print)?
  5. How big are the Christmas, Sallah and wedding-season peaks?
  6. Which brands command a premium, and how much?
  7. What share of your stock is "made in Nigeria", and why?
  8. How has the Chinese-linked trading presence changed your business?

Manufacturers

  1. What is your installed capacity by stage and actual output this year?
  2. What do you pay per kWh across grid, gas and diesel?
  3. What is the age and condition of your key machines?
  4. Where do you buy lint, yarn, greige and dyes, and at what price?
  5. What are your labour costs and productivity per operator?
  6. What legacy liabilities (pensions, debts, tax) attach to the site?
  7. Have you received BOI or CTG fund loans, and on what terms?
  8. Would you consider a JV, lease or sale?

Institutional buyers, associations, consumers

  1. How many uniforms do you procure each year, and to what specification?
  2. How is Executive Order 003 applied in your tenders?
  3. What is your typical payment cycle after delivery?
  4. Who are your current suppliers, and where do they source fabric?
  5. How many members of your association are operating, and at what utilisation?
  6. Which policy change would most help local production?
  7. When will the CTG Development Board begin financing?
  8. What is the status of the Ogun cotton and garment project?
  9. How much do households spend on fabric, tailoring and RMG per year?
  10. When would a consumer pay more for a Nigerian-made fabric?
  11. How has inflation changed your choice between new, used and tailored clothing?
  12. What makes a hotel or hospital switch linen suppliers?

Must-validate before capital

  • Effective landed cost of informal imports versus compliant imports (drives Models B, C and D).
  • Institutional payment cycles and contract volumes (drives Model A working capital).
  • Hotel and modern-trade linen demand (Model E volume).
  • Site power cost achievable with gas or solar in Ogun (all manufacturing models).
  • Legal title and liabilities of any idle mill considered.
So what?

A 10–12 week field programme costing roughly US$250–400k removes most of the uncertainty flagged in this report and is cheap relative to a US$20m+ commitment.

Strategic conclusions and investment recommendations

1
How big is Nigeria's real textile market?≈US$6.5bn (₦8.7tn) at retail in 2026, range US$5.5–8.0bn; ≈US$3.4bn at border value. Official imports capture only about a fifth of supply.
2
Which categories generate the largest sales?New RMG (≈US$1.95bn), polyester woven fabrics (≈US$0.7bn), home textiles (≈US$0.65bn), Ankara prints (≈US$0.6bn) and lace (≈US$0.42bn).
3
Which products depend most on imports?Lace, chiffon, satin, synthetic filament fabrics, suiting, denim and knits (≈98–100%); Ankara (≈90%); RMG (≈88%).
4
Largest operational domestic manufacturers?Sunflag in Lagos; ATM and Tofa in Kano at low output; Chellco in Kaduna (scope to verify). UNTL is the largest asset but idle.
5
Which factories have unused capacity?Effectively all: UNTL Kaduna (idle since 2022), Nichemtex Ikorodu (uncertain), Kano mills (skeletal), and sector-wide utilisation of ≈53%.
6
Which locations dominate?Trading: Lagos Island, Kantin Kwari (Kano), Onitsha. Garment making: Aba and Lagos. Future manufacturing: the Lagos–Ogun corridor.
7
Strongest demand growth?Nonwovens and hygiene (≈7% real), knits and RMG (5–6%), home textiles and uniforms (≈5%).
8
Can domestic manufacturing compete?In cut-and-sew for institutional, bulky and quick-turn goods, yes. In printing, only with tighter enforcement. In spinning and weaving, not on today's energy and fibre costs.
9
Greatest gross-profit potential?Lace and premium prints in trading (cumulative importer-to-retail margin up to ≈45%), uniforms and home textiles in manufacturing (≈27% gross margin).
10
Strongest risk-adjusted returns?Distribution platform (IRR ≈31%), home textiles (≈27%), uniforms (≈21%).
11
Most realistic route to a large business?Distribution first, then conversion plants, then printing, with an ECOWAS-origin sourcing leg; integrate upstream only as volume and policy justify.
12
What to avoid?Greenfield cotton spinning and weaving, integrated mega-mills, large brownfield rehabilitations with inherited liabilities, and any case that depends on an import ban or a new subsidy fund.

  • Constant 2026 US$, 10-year horizon, 18% discount rate, 30% tax, 10-year straight-line depreciation (land excluded), exit at 4.5× Year-10 EBITDA, working capital as % of revenue released at end.
