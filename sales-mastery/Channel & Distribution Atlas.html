Marketing Channels

Channels are the pipelines through which goods and services flow from makers to buyers. Channel management is the core of physical distribution, and channel decisions are some of the most critical in marketing.

An organised network of interconnected organisations and agencies involved in the process of making a product or service available to consumers.Also called middlemen or intermediaries: independent, profit-seeking businesses that sit between the firm and its customers.

Why use middlemen at all?

Direct marketing (no middlemen) keeps control and customer contact. Indirect marketing (1 to n levels) gives reach. People complain that “all they do is raise the price”, yet producers keep using them. Here is why.

÷Fewer transactions

3 makers × 3 buyers = 9 deals direct. With one middleman it drops to 6, cutting effort and cost.

⌖Reach scattered buyers

Producers can't deal one-on-one with consumers spread over a wide area.

₹Money & expertise

Many producers lack the funds and know-how for direct marketing; they can reinvest in their core business instead.

⇄Bridge the gap

Matches buyers' quality, quantity and variety needs with what producers offer.

★Contacts & rapport

Experience, infrastructure and local relationships let them sell more profitably, even unpackaged goods.

⏱Create utilities

Time, place, form and possession utilities: the right product, at the right place, time and pack size.

The 11 functions of a channel

01Facilitation

Bring buyers and sellers together and help close the deal.

02Information

Tell customers about the products and services.

03Promotion

Build and promote the producer's brands.

04Negotiation

Agree prices and delivery terms on the maker's behalf.

05Title transfer

Move ownership from seller to buyer.

06Inventory & risk

Hold ready stock and share its cost and risk.

07Finance

Deposits, advance orders and stock-holding ease the maker's money burden.

08Pre/post-sale service

Demos, installation and maintenance the maker can't deliver to every customer.

09Change agent

Explain price changes and new features and build a favourable opinion of them.

10Warehousing & transport

Store goods and move them to markets and end users.

11Market feedback

Report on customers, competitors and trends, and keep sales records.

Channel levels explorer

Intensity of distribution

Exclusive

Outlets sell only one maker's brand: own showrooms or franchisees. Used for automobiles and luxury lifestyle goods.

Selective

Some chosen outlets, which may also stock rivals. Typical for branded garments and apparel.

Intensive

As many outlets as possible, for FMCG and convenience goods: Pepsi, soap, milk, bread, biscuits.

  • Consumer: who buys, and how
  • Middlemen: cost, suitability, availability, legal limits, upkeep
  • Company: resources, policy, targets, desired coverage
  • Market: nature, size, spread, order size and frequency

  • Catalogue marketing: exclusive and ethnic items

Physical Distribution

A poor distribution system can destroy a good product. More than half of new-product failures have been traced to distribution mistakes rather than technology or market factors.

Planning, implementing and controlling the physical flows of materials and final goods from the place of production to the place of end use to satisfy buyers' needs.Philip Kotler · Logistics is wider: it also covers the inbound flow of raw materials (physical supply).

PProduct ↔ PD

Complex technical products go direct. FMCG needs many intermediaries and warehouses. Perishables need the shortest channel and cold storage.

₹Price ↔ PD

Air freight and extra channel levels push prices up. The relationship runs both ways, and big savings are possible in distribution.

📣Promotion ↔ PD

Ads must be timed with logistics so stock is on the shelf when demand peaks. Co-operative advertising shares cost with channel members.

Kodak vs Polaroid

Kodak's aggressive promotion of its new instant camera built strong demand, but retailers didn't get enough stock. Polaroid stepped in and took the sales. Even loyal buyers won't wait forever.

Objectives: balancing cost and service

ΣTotal cost concept

Lower the overall cost of distribution by balancing transport, warehouse rent, inventory carrying and order-processing costs together, not one at a time.

⚖Cost–service trade-off

More service means more stock, faster transport and more warehouses, so higher cost. Find the level customers expect and competitors offer. Many customers will pay extra for convenience, such as home-delivered movie tickets.

Memory hook · 5 customer-service elements
T · D · C · C · A: “The Delivery Came Complete & Accurate”
Time: short lead / order-cycle time Dependability: consistent lead time, no surprise delays Communication: before, during and after transit Convenience: 24×7, home delivery, ATMs Accuracy: right items, right lots

The 5 components

1Order processing

Receive the order, check credit and dues, check stock, invoice, arrange the transporter, dispatch and inform. GE's system did all of this in under 15 seconds.

2Transportation

Choose the route and the mode. It is the biggest share of distribution cost and decides on-time, undamaged delivery.

3Warehousing

Bridges the time gap between production and consumption, which is vital for seasonal farm produce.

4Material handling

Adds no value but every touch risks damage. Conveyors, forklifts, cranes, hoists; robotic and barcode systems in the West.

5Inventory control

What, when and how much to order and stock at the lowest cost. Tools: JIT and EOQ.

Warehouses

PVPrivate

Owned or leased by the maker. High investment, high control. Big stockists and retail chains.

PBPublic

Rent space only when needed, with grading, labelling and invoicing services. Suits small firms and seasonal demand. FCI and onion “kanda chawls” are examples.

DCDistribution

Short stay, highly automated, goods move out fast. Works like a central dispatch department.

BDBonded

Licensed by customs. Imports wait here until duty is paid; exports wait for shipment.

MDMother depot

Central big stock feeding many smaller warehouses, cutting holding, obsolescence and transport cost.

⌂Location

  • Power, water, labour, transport
  • Room to expand
  • Near highways or rail junctions
  • Municipal rules; outskirts can earn duty concessions

▦Storage methods

  • Use space fully; easy to store, pick and identify
  • FIFO: issue the oldest stock first
  • Like items together; keep items that harm each other apart
  • Heavy items low, fast-movers near the exit

Inventory: JIT and EOQ

JITJust-in-time

Originated in Japan; Tata Motors uses it in India. Material arrives just before production, with no idle stock. It needs reliable suppliers, close communication, attention to quality and good transport. The risk is stock-outs and line shutdowns.

EOQEconomic order quantity

The “least cost quantity”, where ordering cost = carrying cost and total cost is lowest. Ordering cost falls with bulk buying; carrying cost (interest, rent, insurance, obsolescence) rises with it.

Wholesalers

Also called distributors. They buy in bulk from producers and resell in smaller lots to retailers and business users, rarely to the final consumer. In India, millions of small stores make them indispensable.

A business unit which buys and resells merchandise to retailers and other merchants or to industrial, institutional and commercial users, but does not sell in significant amounts to ultimate consumers.American Marketing Association

IDCharacteristics

  • Vital link between maker and retailer
  • Buys bulk, sells small lots
  • Deals in a limited number of products
  • Shows samples instead of displays
  • Operates in a specific area

8 functions

AAssemble & break bulk

Sugar mills sell by the tonne; you buy 1–10 kg. The wholesaler bridges that gap.

WWarehousing

Holds stock and releases it as local demand needs.

GGrading & packing

Sorts by quality into convenient packs; critical for exports.

TTransportation

Moves goods from warehouse to retailers at bulk economy.

FFinancing

Credit to retailers, plus help with accounts and recoveries.

UUninterrupted flow

Adequate stock avoids stock-outs.

RRisk bearing

Owns the goods and carries trade and financial risk.

MMarket information

Knows local trends and competition; helps with research.

“A Wise Grocer Trusts Friends Until Real Money”

Who gains what

→MFor the producer

  • Bulk buying enables mass production
  • Takes over storage and transport burden
  • Opens wide markets with less risk
  • Feeds back market intelligence
  • Prevents stock-outs and brand switching

→RFor the retailer

  • Variety in small lots without big capital
  • Setup and expansion support
  • Steady supply, so lower stock and risk
  • POP material, display and layout ideas
  • Voluntary groups help independents compete with chains

→CFor the consumer

  • Wider choice at the corner shop
  • Emergency needs met, prices stable

4PWholesaler marketing decisions

Target market (serve profitable retailers, discourage others) · Assortment (ABC analysis: A = most profitable) · Pricing (margins flex to clear old stock) · Place (low-rent sites; looks matter little; automation matters) · Promotion (personal selling, co-op advertising).

Exam tip

Growth drivers: India's unorganised retail, distant factories, production ahead of orders. Threats: hypermarkets and consumer co-ops buying direct from makers.

Franchising

The fastest-growing form of retailing and an extended version of licensing. McDonald's is the most admired franchiser in the world.

A form of marketing and distribution in which a parent company (franchiser) grants an individual or small company (franchisee) the right to do business in exchange for fees or royalties.The franchisee pays a lump sum plus a royalty on sales, and runs the outlet by the franchiser's rulebook in a defined territory.

4 types of franchise systems

M→RManufacturer–retailer

Most common. Franchisee sells to consumers.

McDonald's, Raymond

M→WManufacturer–wholesaler

Franchisee bottles and distributes to retailers.

Coca-Cola, Pepsi

W→RWholesaler–retailer

A powerful wholesaler franchises retailers.

Big established wholesalers

S→RService sponsor–retailer

A service firm licenses retailers.

VSNL internet resellers

5 methods of franchising

1Single-unit

One franchisee, one location. The most familiar method.

2Sequential

Extra units granted one at a time, only after proving you can run the existing ones.

3Area development

The “area developer” must open an agreed number of stores within a timeframe.

4Sub-franchising

Master franchisee recruits and supports sub-franchisees in a region.

5Area representation

Like sub-franchising, but the area rep has less responsibility; the franchiser still recruits.

Memory hook · methods, from smallest to widest
“Start Small, Add Some Areas”

Single-unit · Sequential · Area development · Sub-franchising (master) · Area representation

+FFranchiser benefits

  • More territory, rapid growth
  • Steady royalty with little fresh investment
  • Local know-how builds the brand quickly
  • Entry barriers: franchisees act as “category killers”
  • Saves sales-force cost; easier control

+fFranchisee benefits

  • Initial: training, market survey, manuals, finance, staff training
  • Ongoing: central planning, national and co-op ads, audits, POP material, group insurance
  • Proven brand means lower risk and faster returns
  • Banks lend readily against the brand name

−fConstraints on franchisees

  • High entry cost, monthly royalty even in bad months
  • Little freedom; breaking guidelines loses the business
  • Territory encroachment, dual distribution
  • Franchiser's bad decisions or erratic prices
  • Hard exit clauses

♥Keeping the relationship healthy

Avoid misunderstandings · never act against each other · communicate regularly · write clear agreements and territories · share ad costs · treat each other as partners, not dictators · no oversaturation or area-crossing.

Franchising in India

It began with computer training institutes (NIIT, Aptech), then spread to fast food and apparel, starting in South India's IT hubs. Local brands franchise too: Chitale Bandhu sweets and Kaware Ice Creams in Pune. Tanishq has 50+ franchisees in 40 cities.

Managing Channels & Channel Conflict

How to set up and run channels, the integrated systems that replace loose arrangements, and why conflict is natural, sometimes useful, and always manageable.

5 steps of channel management

1Set objectives

Clear goals within resource limits, clearly communicated.

2Decide the pattern

Length, type and number of intermediaries; conventional or not.

3Select members

Judge experience, reputation, contacts, infrastructure, finance, coverage.

4Train & motivate

Factory visits, joint calls, refreshers, rewards. Treat intermediaries as customers.

5Assess & modify

Periodic reviews; add or drop members as the product life cycle moves.

Memory hook · the 5 steps
“Our Pipeline Should Train & Adapt”

Objectives · Pattern · Selection · Training · Assessment

Channel systems

CMSConventional

Loose and unstable. Every member chases its own profit, joins or leaves freely, and conflict is obvious. Used by small makers and for perishables and farm produce.

HMSHorizontal

Many intermediaries at the same level under one structure; channel length 0–1. All-franchisee or all-dealer networks (McDonald's, HPCL), plus strategic alliances such as Maruti + Citibank and GE + Godrej.

VMSVertical

Professionally managed and centrally coordinated, 2+ levels, led by a channel captain. Gives the widest distribution at low cost. HLL, P&G, Nestlé, Ranbaxy.

MMSMultichannel

“Dual distribution”: two or more channels for the same product. Bata, Bajaj, Nike and Ceat Shoppe use own outlets plus dealers; tyre makers sell direct to OEMs and through dealers for replacements.

↕Vertical conflict

Between levels: maker–dealer, wholesaler–retailer, franchiser–franchisee. Caused by goal differences, poor communication, unclear roles.

↔Horizontal conflict

Within the same level: dealer vs dealer. Price-offs, aggressive ads, extra service, territory crossing, malpractice. Price wars can help consumers.

⤨Inter-type

Members trade outside their normal range (“scrambled merchandising”): supermarkets selling vegetables, pan shops selling mobile recharges.

⧉Multichannel

The maker's dual distribution hits the same market: own outlets or direct supply to large retailers undercut dealers, common in readymade garments.

M!When the manufacturer causes it

  • Dual distribution and new channels
  • Oversaturation of dealers
  • Partial treatment and margins
  • No training, joint calls or co-op ads
  • Forced advance orders, dumping stock
  • Late deliveries, no replacement of damaged goods, delayed installations
  • Thin credit, margins never revised

I!When the intermediary causes it

  • Pushes private labels or rival lines
  • Gives rivals more shelf space
  • Ignores the maker's promotions
  • Misses targets, high outstandings
  • Deliberate price-cutting
  • Refuses service and installation
  • No market feedback

7 ways to resolve conflict

1Channel leadership

The member with channel power steers everyone. IBM won over PC dealers by changing commissions, sharing leads and running joint ads.

2Superordinate goals

Agree on a shared big goal: survival, share, quality, satisfaction.

3Exchange of persons

Swap staff between levels to see the other side's view.

4Co-optation

Bring the other side's leaders onto your advisory councils or boards.

5Trade associations

Joint membership brings everyone under one roof.

6Diplomacy

Standing envoys who work continuously with counterparts.

7Third party

Mediation: a neutral helps the parties reach their own deal. Arbitration: the parties accept the arbitrator's decision, which can be compulsory.

Exam tip

Mediation persuades and keeps parties talking; the parties decide. Arbitration decides for them. Examiners often ask for this difference in one line.

Multi-Level Marketing

A controversial non-store, direct-selling channel. Also called network marketing, party selling, or pyramid selling. Independent contractors earn on their own sales and on the sales of everyone they recruit.

▲How the pyramid works

Company
Master distributors
Downline distributors
Consumers (and stuck last links)

Pay a registration fee, buy fixed lots in advance, sell or recruit. If you can do neither, you end up the consumer. No ads, no displays: the network is the promotion.

⌂Party plan vs MLM

Party plan: a host invites friends; products are demonstrated; the host earns a gift or commission. MLM: multi-tier non-employee distributors who sell and recruit.

Who uses it: Amway, Avon, Oriflame (pioneer in India, with Rollscon), Modicare, Tupperware, Electrolux, Britannica.

What sells: light, portable products that need no after-sales service, such as cosmetics, personal care, kitchenware, books and toys. Trial kits and testers help.

+Benefits

  • Home convenience for buyers
  • Income for homemakers, retirees, youth
  • No distribution network cost or liability for the maker
  • No ad spend: distributors promote
  • Network self-expands; move to new areas when one saturates
  • High distributor margins drive sales

−Drawbacks

  • Limited choice, higher prices
  • Hard to exchange faulty goods
  • Slow to start, unpredictable growth
  • Unfit for technical or service-heavy products
  • At saturation, the last links are stuck with stock
  • Pushy tactics, scam firms, lost friendships
Memory hook · the name says it
MLM = “Make Lots of Money”, until the pyramid runs out of people

It succeeds only while new recruits keep joining. The bottom layer always pays for saturation.

Supply Chain Management

“Competition today is not among individual businesses but among their networks. The winner is the company with the better network.” SCM reaches further back than channels: right to the raw-material supplier.

The integration of businesses from end user through original suppliers that provides products, services, and information that add value for customers.A cradle-to-grave approach managing the flow of material, information and funds.
Raw-material suppliers→Logistics→Manufacturer→Logistics→Intermediaries / retailers→Consumers

Goods flow forward; information flows back. That return flow is what makes a chain “smart”.

Cases worth remembering

Wal-Mart × P&G

A shared system alerts P&G whenever its products run low, right down to scans at the till. Less inventory and automatic invoicing let P&G fund “everyday low prices”.

Levi's

Every night Levi's learns which sizes sold at Sears, orders fabric for next-day delivery, and the fabric maker orders fibre from DuPont. Network vs network: Levi's vs Wrangler.

Walgreens × Colgate

A toothpaste scan triggers automatic replenishment. Colgate even pre-tags prices, so stock goes straight onto the shelf.

FoodWorld (RPG)

India's first national supermarket chain sources fresh produce directly from farmers and runs a consolidation centre at Hosekote: about 250 tonnes a month, 32 vegetable types.

Fashion retail

Many short seasons mean the chain must re-order fast-sellers and re-dye or re-cut slow styles mid-season.

Citibank Singapore

Places linked computers in corporate clients' offices, bringing the bank into the client's workflow.

⚙Running the chain efficiently

Customer-service standards and benchmarking · Transport (mode, consolidation, routing, scheduling) · Inventory (stocking policy, JIT, push and pull) · Warehousing (layout, docks, handling) · Purchase (source and timing) · Information maintenance.
