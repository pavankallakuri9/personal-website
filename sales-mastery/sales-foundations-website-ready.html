The whole journey from a cold name on a list to a customer who buys again and again. Each tab is one stage, with its own colour, the frameworks to know, phrases you can use, and a memory box at the end.

00

What selling is

Selling happens when a salesperson, in a personal interaction, helps a potential buyer decide to buy a product or service. It is one element of the promotional mix, next to advertising, sales promotion, direct marketing and public relations, and the only one that talks back.

Marketing

Make what the customer wants

  • Focus on the customer
  • Meets needs and wants
  • Shapes the product to the market
vs
Selling

Help the customer want what you have

  • Focus on the product
  • Persuades face to face
  • Adjusts the message to each buyer
Shared goalBoth aim at customer satisfaction. That makes every salesperson a micro-marketer for one customer at a time.

Three goals of selling

1

Find

Find new prospects who need what you sell.

2

Convince

Help those prospects decide to buy.

3

Keep

Keep customers satisfied so they buy again and tell others.

Why personal selling matters

Strengths
  • The message can change on the spot to answer each buyer's questions.
  • It targets the most promising leads with precision.
  • You read spoken cues and body language and adapt.
  • Two-way flow: feedback on customers, products and competitors comes straight back.
  • It builds lasting relationships with customers.
Limit

Cost per sale is high

A sales force is expensive to hire, train and run. That is why the rest of this guide is about making every call count.

Topic 01 · The seller

Who sells, and what great looks like

Your job is not to push a product. It is to solve problems and help customers make good buying decisions, while keeping an eye on competitors and the market.

Big ideaMove up the value chain: from order taker, to problem solver, to trusted advisor.
01

Three types of salespeople

Grouped by what they do. In practice one person often does all three jobs.

T

Order takers

Handle routine and repeat orders, restock, arrange displays and answer calls. They generate the bulk of a firm's sales but get less training and pay, so turnover is high.

Inside: from the store or phoneField: visit the customer
G

Order getters

Drive growth by winning new customers and selling more to existing ones. Used for expensive, complex or brand-new products. Under target pressure, so they need constant training and motivation.

Current customer salesNew business sales
S

Support personnel

Don't close sales but make them possible: find prospects, educate buyers, build goodwill and give after-sales help. Common in business-to-business selling.

MissionaryTradeTechnical
New business is harderConverting a stranger into a buyer takes longer than selling more to a current customer. The two most common routes in: referrals from existing customers and door-to-door outreach.

Selling then and now

Then

One-way persuasion

Tell the customer everything about the product and why your company is best. The standard pitch is now seen as the lowest level of selling.

→
Now

Partnership and problem solving

Buyers are informed and demanding. They expect you to understand their business and bring solutions to problems they didn't know they had.

Six traits of top salespeople

There is no single formula, but strong sellers are natural problem solvers who share these habits.

Thick skin

Even the best lose more often than they win. They handle rejection without taking it personally.

Willing to take risks

They try unproven ideas and go into new territory.

Generosity

They share credit freely, without fear of looking smaller.

Methodical

Planning and follow-up are where sales are won.

Tenacity

Effort and determination to tackle hard problems.

Resourcefulness

Always improving the approach and using the talent around them.

85%of top salespeople in one survey were conscientious
82%of top salespeople are inquisitive: they ask questions

The value ladder

Where do you sit with each customer? Value only starts when you help them work out what they need.

Supplier

Fills orders. Reacting, adding little value.

Solution provider

Solves known problems. Still reactive.

Needs partner

Helps the customer find their real needs.

Trusted advisor

Called for ideas, insight and help. Rock-solid position.

86%of B2B buyers expect sellers to be trusted advisors

Remember

  1. Order takers keep business flowing; order getters grow it; support staff make it possible.
  2. The canned pitch is the lowest level of selling today.
  3. Your goal with every customer is to become the person they call for advice.
Take · Get · Support

Topic 02 · Why buyers buy

Inside the buyer's mind

Buying decisions come down to three things buyers look for in a seller: you know your stuff, you understand them, and they can trust you.

Big ideaTrust takes a long time to build and a second to lose.
02

The three buying behaviours

1

Believe in your position

People buy from people who know their stuff. Know your product inside out, and also the customer's business, industry, competitors and market. Most of it is online.

2

Empathy

Seeing things from the buyer's side. For many buyers, a seller who understands their situation is the single strongest reason to buy. Empathy does not depend on age or background: let them talk and show real interest.

3

Trust

Be responsive, direct, clear, reliable and straightforward. Assume the customer is smart, keep promises, and never spring nasty surprises.

Watch for biasAffinity bias pulls you toward customers who are like you and away from those who aren't. It can keep you from talking to the people who actually control the decision.

The trust formula

Being seen as trustworthy is not the same as being trusted with a large contract. This formula shows the gap.

Trust= ( Credibility×Intimacy )÷Risk
Credibility · you control

You know what you are talking about and you deliver.

Intimacy · you control

How safe it feels to work with you: empathy, warmth, sensitivity, likeability.

Risk · they feel

How much the buyer could lose. Higher risk divides your trust down, so work to shrink it.

Eight ways to lower the buyer's risk

Keep asking yourself: how can I make saying yes less risky for this customer?

Start small

Ask for a piece of the business, not all of it. Earn more over time.

Show a parallel

Walk through a similar case and how it worked.

Build an exit

Show there is a way out if things don't go to plan.

Reassure

You'll be there throughout, ready to act if anything goes wrong.

Guarantee

Offer assurance, or at least your commitment to see it through.

Share the risk

Explain what you lose if it fails. "We're in this together."

Take the burden

Take full responsibility if things go wrong.

Explain the worst case

Name all the risks and how you'll keep them under control.

Gain respect by showing it

Their time

Reply promptly and concisely.

Their business

Ask before you take notes. Respect confidentiality.

Your competitors

Running down their current supplier insults the person who chose it.

Stand out: the "wow" checklist

Give customers something they didn't ask for or expect. When you and a competitor are neck and neck, this tips the scale.

A relevant article or piece of research you could send?

A third party who can provide something you can't?

A supplier who could help them cut costs?

A pressing problem you can help them solve?

A personal milestone you could acknowledge?

A useful conference or event for them?

Stay visibleVisit in person when you can: you learn needs directly, read body language and see who they work with. Stay on their mind with short notes and messages, without becoming a nuisance.

Remember

  1. Buyers buy on credibility, empathy and trust.
  2. Trust = (Credibility × Intimacy) ÷ Risk. You raise the top; you shrink the bottom.
  3. Never knock a competitor. It insults the customer's past choice.
C×I ÷ R

Say it as "Customers Invest when Risk falls." Top line is your job, bottom line is their fear.

Topic 03 · The selling process

Seven steps from stranger to customer

The aim is customer satisfaction and a long-term relationship. Some situations use all seven steps; a repeat customer lets you skip prospecting and pre-approach.

Big ideaThe process is easy to understand and hard to do. Planning before and after the meeting is what separates good from excellent.
03

The seven steps

tap a step

Where leads come from

People

Current and former customers, suppliers, dealers, colleagues, friends and their networks.

Events

Social events, seminars, conferences, trade shows and exhibitions.

Published info

Directories, news and trade press, databases, public records, mailing lists, online articles and social media.

Associations

Professional bodies, trade associations and chambers of commerce.

Qualify: is this a real prospect?

Don't spend time on everyone. Check three things, then sort leads by temperature.

Willingness

Will they?

Is there an immediate or future need they want to meet?

Ability

Can they?

Can they afford it? Check credit ratings or annual reports for firms.

Authority

May they?

Are they allowed to make the decision?

Cold lead

Can't turn into a customer. Move on.

Warm lead

Has a hidden or future need. Nurture.

Hot lead

Willing to buy. Plan the call now.

Talk to the right personTwo-thirds of sales calls go to people who don't make or carry out decisions. Ask: "Everyone has their own way of making decisions. So I don't leave anyone out of the loop, how does the process work here?"

Plan with five questions

Who?

Who decides? Who should I see? Who does what on our side?

What?

What will I ask? What drives their decisions? What objections do I expect?

Where & when?

In person or online? When is the most effective time?

Why?

Why this customer? Why is this approach good for us?

How?

How will I run the meeting and stand out from competitors?

RehearsePractise your questions, objection answers and even the close with colleagues. It shows where you are shaky and where information is missing.

StyleHow it worksBest forWatch out
CannedA memorised talk, the same for every buyer (stimulus–response)Simple products, new sellers, door-to-door and phone sellingBuyer can't raise questions; feels frustrating
FormulatedIdentify needs and buying style, then try one appeal after anotherModerately complex salesStill seller-led; can miss the real need

First contact: three emails

Introduce

A formal note about you and your company. A printed letter can stand out.

Confirm

Date, time, length, agenda and useful reading. Invite others who should attend.

Remind

A short reminder before the meeting.

Follow up

After the meeting: thanks, summary, next steps.

First meeting: do
  • Confirm in writing
  • Be humble: you haven't worked together yet
  • Show what you learned in preparation
  • Thank them for their time
  • Ask lots of questions and let them talk
First meeting: don't
  • Turn up unprepared
  • Act overly familiar
  • Treat it like any other meeting
  • Act as if you're entitled to it
  • Present specific recommendations yet

Running the sales meeting

You called the meeting, but it belongs to the customer. Your role is facilitator.

Build rapport

Introductions, small talk, a sincere compliment, a news item about their business. Move on at their pace.

Set the agenda

Ask each person what they want from the meeting. Confirm the time and stick to it.

Guide

Keep to the purpose, draw ideas from everyone, move to an action plan and book the follow-up.

Salesperson

Facilitates, may take minutes, helps find solutions.

Client

Owns the problem, may chair, helps find solutions.

Others

Help find good solutions and bring problem-solving resources.

Remember

  1. Prospect → Pre-approach → Approach → Present → Handle objections → Close → Follow up.
  2. Qualify every lead: Will they? Can they? May they?
  3. Presentations follow AIDA: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action.
PPAPHCF

"Please Prepare A Perfect Handshake, Close, Follow." One word per step, in order.

Topic 04 · Needs-based selling

Find the need before the answer

Determine a customer's needs before you propose anything. If you respond to the first need you hear, it is like judging a book by its first chapter.

Big ideaPeople don't want a quarter-inch drill. They want a quarter-inch hole.
04
80%of firms think they deliver a superior experience
8%of firms actually do, say their customers
63%of business buyers say sales talks focus on products, not solutions
84%of B2B buyers prefer sellers who understand their goals

Selling is problem solving

The two follow almost the same steps. If you're good at one, you'll be good at the other.

StepProblem solvingNeeds-based selling
01Set the stage and give the session structureOpen the meeting: rapport, agenda, prepare the customer
02Define the problem and what's been triedDetermine needs, both obvious and hidden
03Generate ideas without judgingPresent products through features and benefits
04Evaluate: list what appeals, then the concernsResolve objections sensitively
05Summarise the solution as an action planClose the deal and agree next steps

Two kinds of need

Every buyer, however company-minded, is also driven by personal needs. Risk-averse buyers, and those worried about how they look, lean heavily on personal needs.

Business needs · measurable
  • Reduce cost
  • Increase efficiency
  • Shorten production time
  • Increase profitability
  • Improve turnaround time
+
Personal needs · subjective
  • Look good in front of peers
  • Gain recognition
  • Get that promotion
  • Minimise personal risk
  • Decrease stress
i

Read between the lines

A client complains that his boss keeps second-guessing him. The implied need: a solid, well-reasoned case he can use to defend the purchase. Overt needs anyone can hear. Implied needs are where you stand out.

The 5 : 3 : 1 question mix

Keep it a conversation, not an interrogation. A comfortable ratio is five fact-finding questions, three needs-oriented, one big-picture.

5
Fact-finding

Basic facts. Everyone asks these; they don't set you apart, but you can't skip them.

  • What are your annual sales?
  • Who are your current suppliers?
  • How often do you buy?
  • Who makes the decisions?
3
Needs-oriented

Open-ended. Get them talking about goals, hopes, problems and fears. These set you apart.

  • What do you expect from someone like me?
  • What are your biggest challenges today?
  • If you could change one thing about how you work, what would it be?
1
Big-picture

Strategic. Uncovers needs they don't know they have and makes you an advisor. Plan these carefully.

  • Where would you like the company to be in five years?
  • What could stop that from happening?
  • What is your vision for the business?
Give a preamble

Tell them questions are coming, why, and how answering helps them get better recommendations.

Cluster topics

Group questions (strategy, finance, stock), each with its own short intro.

Careful with "why"

It can sound judgemental. Try: "I'd like to know more about the process behind the decision to…"

!

The pad-and-pen pitch

A challenger drinks brand wanted an airline's in-flight business and couldn't win on price. Instead of a polished deck, the team turned up with only notepads and spent two hours asking questions. They learned the airline's biggest need, after safety, was selling more tickets. Their winning idea: discount coupons for airline tickets through retailers. The airline switched suppliers and saw millions in extra sales within a year.

Keep askingLong relationships breed complacency: you start assuming instead of asking, and your share quietly falls. The person who understands the most needs wins the business.

Listen like it's your job (it is)

We think faster than people speak, so attention drifts to the next question, tomorrow's meeting, the drive home.

Active listening

Attend, then reflect back

Give the speaker full attention, then repeat in your own words what you heard, including the feeling behind it.

Make notes, don't just take them

Pen on paper from minute one

Capture each need, concern and opportunity, plus the links you spot. It stops you answering too early, and people often save their deepest need for last.

✎

"Bring two pads for our guests"

Two sellers sat through the start of a meeting without writing anything. The client phoned his assistant for two pads and pens, "so I can have the impression they are at least somewhat interested." Four out of five clients think a seller who doesn't write isn't engaged. Always ask first if it's OK to take notes.

Review needs before you present

Showing you understood is a sale in itself. Many customers decide to buy at this point. Tell them what you heard, not what they said.

Say
  • "Here's my understanding of what you said…"
  • "I may be reading too much into this, but it appears that…"
  • "How I interpreted that was that you'd like to…"
Avoid
  • "What you need is…"
  • "You said that…"
  • "X told us you wanted…"

Overt needs first

Summarise what they stated.

Then implied

Add what you inferred.

Confirm

Check with every person in the room.

Prioritise

Ask which matter most and what you missed.

Solve it together

Brainstorm

Wild first, tame later

Define the task, time and roles. Then invite every idea and judge none. It is easier to tone down a wild idea than to invent a new one. Don't over-define the problem or people stop speculating.

Evaluate

Positives, then concerns

Pick the best ideas. List what appeals first so it is preserved, then fix each concern, worst first, until the idea works. Finish with an action plan.

Bringing a colleague? Five benefits of a joint call
Specialist knowledgeTwo pairs of earsFeedback on your performanceDifferent perspectivesShows the client they matter

Brief them on the client, the goal and their role. Introduce them with why they're there: "She has worked on problems like this for 15 years."

Remember

  1. Never present until you understand the need. Humility here is a strength.
  2. Ask 5 fact, 3 need, 1 big-picture question.
  3. Make notes. Review what you heard. Confirm with everyone.
5 · 3 · 1

"Five facts, three feelings, one future." Facts set the scene, feelings find the need, the future makes you an advisor.

Topic 05 · Recommending solutions

Answer "so what?"

Presenting is the part most salespeople enjoy. It works when every feature is tied to a benefit, and every benefit is tied to this customer's confirmed need.

Big ideaPeople buy benefits, not features. Lead with what's in it for them.
05

AIDA: the shape of a presentation

A

Attention

Open with something that makes them look up.

I

Interest

Let them touch, hold and try it while you talk.

D

Desire

Show how it meets their needs, plus extra benefits.

A

Action

Make the next step clear and easy.

Three questions every pitch must answer

What? · Feature

How it works: specs, attributes, facts. Easy to say, hard to argue with.

So what? · Benefit

Why it matters and how it helps. More subjective, and the reason people buy.

What's in it for me? · Specific benefit

How it meets this customer's confirmed, top-priority need. This sets you apart.

FeatureBenefit statement
Neat designSaves space, so it's more convenient
Solid constructionLasts longer, which saves you money
Ergonomic featuresBetter performance and comfort for the user
Reinforced materialsEasy to carry and robust, giving more flexibility
Half the storyA feature without its benefit tells only half the story. Link them every time: "…which means that you…"

Order the pitch: specific first

Peak listening lasts about 90 seconds before it drops sharply. Specific benefits close deals, so get them in early.

Review needs

Short recap of what you heard.

Recommend

Your solution.

Specific benefits

How it meets their top needs.

Generic features & benefits

The brochure material, last.

Hold back the brochureHand it over after you present, not before, and highlight the parts that match their needs.

Give ideas, not just products

What's in your head matters as much as what's in your bag. A free, useful idea shows you value the relationship, even if they don't use it.

Where to look
  • Could better technology or software help them?
  • Problems they keep raising that you could research
  • A cheaper way to do something they already do
  • A book or podcast linked to a shared interest
  • A seminar or course to build their industry knowledge
How to offer it
  • Ask permission: "I've been thinking about your situation and have an idea. Would it be useful to share?"
  • State the need you see, present the idea, explain the benefit
  • Be humble and credit others
  • Save it for the end of the meeting to finish on a high
✉

The article that won the deal

Three suppliers bid for a training company's video system and were level on price, quality and service. One salesperson emailed a news article about one of the client's own customers with a note: "I'm sure you saw this, but just in case…" That seller won the contract.

Ask for feedback

Nodding heads don't mean yes. If you don't ask, you don't learn. Expect an objection, and welcome it: an objection out in the open can be resolved.

What do you think about our recommendation?How does that sound?I've been talking for a while; now I'd love to hear from you.
AvoidSlippery patter like "Sounds pretty good, doesn't it?" It feels manipulative and invites a blunt no.

Remember

  1. Feature = what. Benefit = so what. Specific benefit = what's in it for me.
  2. Lead with specifics; you have about 90 seconds of full attention.
  3. End by asking for feedback with an open question.
What → So what? → Me?

Picture the customer asking those three questions out loud after every sentence you say.

Topic 06 · Handling objections

An objection is a request for more

Most people object even when they want to buy. They are looking for reassurance that they are making a sensible decision. Silence is the worse sign: it often means no interest at all.

Big ideaMost objections are unmet needs in disguise, and most show some level of interest.

Why buyers object

Cognitive dissonance

Two thoughts at once

Part of the buyer feels good about the purchase and part isn't sure. Objecting protects them from feeling hasty. It's the same reason people read car ads after they've bought the car.

Common reasons
  • No time to talk right now
  • No money or authority
  • Product doesn't fit their need
  • A poor experience during the presentation
  • They want more information
  • Habit, or to test the seller

Three reactions that kill deals

01 · Aggressive

Arguing

Implies you're right and they're wrong. Shuts down openness.

02 · Defensive

Justifying

Makes the conversation about you, not the client.

03 · Passive

Giving up

The worst reaction. There may be real interest behind the objection.

The resolution process

Borrowed from conflict management. You don't have to use every step, but a clear process stops you reacting too fast.

Acknowledge

Recognise their right to object, without agreeing or disagreeing.

Clarify

Ask open, content-free questions to hear the whole issue.

Reframe

Turn the objection into a need you can meet. Confirm it.

Respond

Address the need with your strongest evidence.

Check for more

"Is there anything else we need to discuss?" Repeat, or close.

1 · Acknowledge
That's a fair question…I appreciate your candour…I can see why…I recognise that cost is a major concern for you.
2 · Clarify, without leading the witness
Could you be more specific?Can you say a little more about that?How come?I'm not sure I understand. Could you clarify?
Don't plant doubtsIf the objection is vague, never ask "Is it the price?" You've just given them a new reason not to buy.
Be positive

"Thank you. That's very helpful."

Be direct

"I'd like to ask another question or two so that…"

Mirror

"Ridiculous?" → "What makes it seem ridiculous?" Said with genuine curiosity.

Be silent

When a reaction is too strong, a pause can defuse it.

Create confidence: similar problems you've solved.

Review features & benefits.

Price: the most common objection

Pre-empt it

Ask about budget during discovery. If they won't say, give a "sense of" cost: "Programmes like this typically cost about 100. How does that sound?"

Find what's really behind it

Price is often a smokescreen. Ask "How far off are we?" and compare the value of competing offers, not just the number.

Reframe to value

"So you need to see exactly what you get for the extra 10 per cent." Price is not value; people pay more when they see the value.

Never just drop the priceCutting price without removing something says you overcharged. If you must lower it, take something off the table, or make clear it's a one-off to earn your way in.

Six objection types and what to do

NNeed · "We're happy as we are"+
I'm not interestedWe don't need itI don't see how it helps

Don't kill the deal. Dig deeper: is it real satisfaction, or inertia and fear of change? Help them see the size of the problem or opportunity first, and only then position your product. Lower the barrier with trials and pilots.

MMoney · "It's too expensive"+
No budget right nowBudget is needed elsewhereWe'll buy if you cut the price

Ask qualifying questions: are they unsure of the value, truly short of budget, or haggling? Show the return on investment clearly. A tight budget needn't end the deal; it may just need creative pricing.

Leaving the price aside for a moment, is this the right solution for you?
TTime · "Call me in two months"+
I'm busy nowNot a focus for usI need time to think

Respect their priorities, then make your issue one of them: show the problem behind it and why it matters now. Tie your product to their top three goals, or find someone in the company for whom it is a priority.

What are your top three priorities this quarter?
TrTrust · "Never heard of you"+
I've read bad reviewsWe had a bad experienceI heard about your problems

Don't take it personally. If you're new, explain who you are and share case studies and success stories. For bad press, hear them out fully without interrupting, then explain what you've done to fix it.

I'd like to hear what you've heard, then tell you what we changed.
PProduct · "It's too complicated"+
I don't understand itI need features X and YWe'll buy if you add…

Peel the onion one layer at a time. Start with the number-one problem it solves for them and reach the "aha" moment before the extras. For feature requests, check: nice-to-have or deal-breaker? There is often a workaround.

If that feature existed tomorrow, what would it let you do?
CCompetitor · "We already use X"+
How are you different?I'm happy with XX is cheaper

Every product has trade-offs. Find what they live with today and what it would take to get a foot in the door, perhaps with a different team or use case. Point out real differences, but never rubbish the competitor.

If you could improve one thing about your current setup, what would it be?

Topic 07 · Closing the sale

If you don't ask, you don't get

Closing should be the natural end of a good problem-solving conversation, not a trick. The biggest barrier is fear of rejection, so practise until asking feels normal.

Big ideaIf the customer has no reason not to buy, assume they're ready, and ask.
07

The cleanest close

Same credibility and honesty as the rest of the process. Check for concerns, double-check, then ask.

You

"Anything else we need to discuss?"

Customer

"No, not that I can think of."

You

"So everything seems OK?"

Customer → You

"Yes, I believe so." → "Great, then how do we get started?"

Trial closeTest readiness during the presentation with small questions about payment terms or delivery. Their answers show how close they are.

24 closing techniques

"What took you so long to ask?"

A young seller visited a client twice a month for two years. On the big day he reviewed prices, terms and specs and kept asking if everything was fine. It was. He still couldn't ask for the order. His manager finally said "Well then, how about an order?" The customer replied, "What took you so long to ask?" Hesitation raises suspicion. Ask.

Topic 08 · Beyond the close

The real selling starts after the yes

Anyone can win a first order with hard work. The successful ones win the second, third and fourth. Don't be the seller who disappears once the contract is signed.

Big ideaAlways Be Closing? Better: Always Be Collecting. Keep questioning, understanding and solving together.

6–7×more expensive to win a new customer than keep one
66%of people who switch brands do so because of poor service

Follow up vs follow through

Follow up

Do what you promised

Make sure samples arrive, delivery dates are met, the team turns up. Every call, even a short catch-up, gets a follow-up message: thanks, summary, next steps, who does what by when.

→
Follow through

Do more than expected

Check progress internally every day. Send a dated list of deliverables and hit every one. Warn early if you'll miss one. Send updates that need no reply.

S

Satisfaction

Was it delivered on time? Any problems after installation?

D

No dissonance

Learn their future needs early.

Relationship marketing

Keeping customers is far more profitable than finding new ones. Selling to existing customers skips prospecting, rapport-building and research. Some sellers even recommend a rival's product when it fits the customer better, and earn lasting trust.

Thank-you note

For the order and for any referrals.

Useful information

Articles about their market and competitors.

New launches

With a note on how it could help them.

Offers

Upcoming sales, special deals, discount coupons.

Reminders

Pending orders and reorder dates.

Personal touches

Greetings on birthdays and holidays, a small branded gift.

Making the mark

Do
  • Promise, then deliver
  • Put it in writing
  • Stay visible
  • Be consistently professional
  • Show interest in more business
  • Track every contact in a simple log
Don't
  • Over-promise and under-deliver
  • Assume the customer understands
  • Become a nuisance
  • Forget details
  • Look desperate or over-anxious

  2. Every call deserves a follow-up message.
  3. Track your contacts so 90 days never slip by.

Topic 09 · Sales force management

Building a team that sells

Plan, implement, evaluate. Set objectives, design and size the team, hire and train the right people, pay them well, and keep them motivated.

Big ideaHire too many and profit falls. Hire too few and morale and sales fall.
09

Objectives and quotas

Team objectives

What the team aims to achieve in a period

Stated as sales volume, market share and overall profit. Set with the salespeople, starting from key performance areas.

Individual quotas

Measurable targets per person

  • Unit sales volume and average order size
  • Number of calls and order ratio
  • Dealers contacted, displays and demos
  • New accounts opened
Make them explicit, feasible, measurableQuotas reflect the selling objectives, sales plan, team size and territory, and motivate when they're clearly communicated.

Three ways to organise

ByHowPlusMinus
Territory · whereEach seller owns an area, from part of a city to a countrySimplest; know the market, less travel, stronger relationshipsWeak for complex products needing specialists
Customer · whoSplit by customer type, e.g. institutions vs householdsFits different needs and requirementsCostlier: several sellers cover one area
Product · whatSpecialists per product lineDeep product knowledgeSeveral sellers call on the same customer

How many salespeople?

Equalized workload

Everyone carries the same load. Group customers by purchase volume, set calls needed per group, then divide by calls one person can make.

Reps = total annual calls needed ÷ calls per rep

Limits: call needs vary by customer, and travel distances differ.

Incremental productivity · most appropriate

Keep adding a salesperson as long as the extra sales they bring exceed the cost of hiring them. Needs forecasts of sales, selling costs and hiring cost.

Recruit and select

Define first

Set the qualifications and write a job description listing the tasks.

Internal or external

Promote your own sellers, move staff across, or recruit via referrals, job fairs, adverts, agencies and campuses.

Select carefully

Tests check aptitude, language and problem solving; interviews check drive, maturity and stress tolerance. A wrong hire costs training, lost sales and a re-hire.

Routing and schedulingSet territory, customer count and spread, then call frequency, length and transport, to get the most from each call at the least time and cost.

Train

Sales-oriented

Pressure and presentations

Assumes buyers need pushing and won't complain later. Little care for satisfaction or after-sales.

vs
Customer-oriented

Solve problems

Assumes every customer has unique, hidden needs. Ask probing questions and give honest advice. Builds trust.

What new hires learn

Company policies, products, warranties, terms, promotion and distribution.

What veterans learn

New products, policy changes, advanced techniques, time management.

Where

In the field, at educational institutions, or in company learning centres.

Tell me, I forget. Show me, I remember. Involve me, I understand.

Role play · most effective

One plays the seller, one the customer, others observe. The seller reflects on their own performance, observers say what worked, the trainer sums up. Repeat until everyone has played.

Evaluate training

Compare sales records or test scores before and after, or send observers into the field. Effects take time to show, and theory doesn't always become practice.

Pay

Straight salary

Security

Fixed, predictable, simple. Good for new recruits and long industrial sales cycles. Weak incentive to hit targets.

Straight commission

Drive

A percentage of sales or profit. Suits experienced sellers. Too low or too complex and it demotivates; costs more to administer.

Salary + commission · most preferred

Both

Security of a base plus the drive to earn more. Flexible and motivating.

Motivate and evaluate

Sellers spend most of their time away from the office, so belonging and loyalty can fade. Link their personal goals to the company's.

Real-play

Everyone puts a token in a bowl and phones real prospects. First to book an appointment wins the bowl.

Assignments

Ask a seller to present a topic at the next meeting, especially one they're weak in.

Contests

Most sellers love a competition.

Two solutions rule

Bring every problem with two possible solutions. Builds judgement.

Financial incentives
  • Salary
  • Bonus
  • Commission
+
Non-financial incentives
  • Recognition and public praise
  • Training, flexibility, authority
  • Prizes, promotion, job security
  • A personal call from the top boss
EvaluationA periodic appraisal compares results with objectives, using call reports, customer feedback and invoices. Falling short? Adjust standards, add training and supervision, or improve pay and motivation.

Remember

  1. Organise by Where (territory), Who (customer) or What (product).
  2. Size: equal workload for a quick estimate; incremental productivity for the right answer.
  3. Salary = security, commission = drive, combination = both.
